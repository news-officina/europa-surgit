Denmark had the luck. Europe needs the plan.

Gefion is approaching full capacity and gained a new owner as demand is expected to rise at least sixfold within five years. One Danish supercomputer is luck; a European network of them, owned across borders, would be a plan.

14 November 2026

Denmark's AI supercomputer Gefion just gained an owner that already runs public-sector systems abroad. Gefion, a supercomputer with 1,528 Nvidia chips, went live in 2024, funded with about DKK 700 million, roughly €94 million, from the Novo Nordisk Foundation and Denmark's state investment fund EIFO. It runs on renewable energy. DCAI wants it to serve public institutions and businesses whose data is sensitive or regulated. This week it grew. On 10 November 2026 the software firm Netcompany became a joint owner of the company behind Gefion, the Danish Centre for AI Innovation, in a capital raise of about DKK 500 million, around €67 million. That model should not stay a Danish first. The Commission and the national funds should copy it into one European AI compute platform.

This deal shows where one country's money runs out. Gefion is approaching full capacity, as DCAI's chairperson Nana Bule said when the deal was signed, and EIFO's chief executive Peder Lundquist expects demand for AI capacity in Denmark to rise at least sixfold within five years. The new DKK 500 million goes to that machine's infrastructure and its operations. The machine needs additional capital and competencies for sovereign and secure AI, in the chairperson's words. And the buyers DCAI has in mind are public institutions and businesses that need AI capacity they can trust where data is sensitive or regulated, as its chief executive Martin Svensson says. The Commission should size its AI compute money to that gap.

One Gefion is Denmark's luck. A network of them is Europe's.

Netcompany brings the missing piece, and it is European already. The sceptics say Gefion is a Danish deal of foundation, state and company money, so Brussels owes it no European copy. The people who own it answer that themselves. The Novo Nordisk Foundation, DCAI's majority owner, is open to bringing on additional owners. And the market analysis DCAI cites points to demand many times the company's current scale. Demand that size outgrows any one privately funded machine. Keep that structure, lose the border. Five years from now you should tap compute like this wherever in the Union you work. The platforms should be owned by several member states, foundations and firms sharing capacity. That is the scale Denmark's luck was pointing at all along.

Denmark's own ministers already made the argument for us. Research minister Christina Egelund says Europe must be able to do much more on its own and Denmark must contribute to European technological sovereignty. Business minister Martin Lidegaard calls the investment a contribution to Europe's digital sovereignty. Hold them to their words: a sovereignty announced in Hellerup should be built at continental scale. EU compute money should go only to those who adopt the DCAI structure across borders, making the Danish model the entry ticket rather than the endpoint. Fund the network, not one machine. And write to your own research minister: ask them to take the Danish words to Brussels.
