One return accord for twenty-seven beats five separate deals

The Union built the return instrument and then let five governments run it alone. Europe's job deserves Europe's policy: one negotiated accord, open to all.

16 November 2026

Europe just built the tool, then handed it to five. Fewer than three in ten people ordered to leave the Union actually leave, the confirmed figure behind the promises you will hear about this. Earlier in 2026 the Union approved a reform that allows return hubs. That was the instrument, ours, all of ours. And then, in Vienna on 16 November, Germany, Austria, Denmark, Greece and the Netherlands presented their return hub in Rwanda, first transfers expected in 2027, costs carried by the five themselves. Take their scheme and make it one accord, negotiated by the European Commission and open to all twenty-seven. That is the road.

Separate budgets, separate rules, a five-country club where a common policy belongs. Rescue, one of the signatory organisations, says Rwanda fails the very test the new Return Regulation sets, since it only allows agreements with countries respecting international human rights standards and the prohibition of refoulement. The answer sits in the rule itself: one independent verification of the partner against that standard before a single person transfers, done once, in public, for the whole Union. The accord is not with Rwanda. And notice the record: in January 2026, four of these five governments themselves raised concerns about Rwandan detention conditions at its UN review.

One accord turns the risk into a check.

Done as Europe's instrument, this becomes infrastructure. The Commission already sat at the Vienna table, as observer, contributing to how the provisions could be implemented in line with EU law. Convert that seat into a mandate: one accord agreed with the Council, one set of legal safeguards, and the rule the Return Regulation already carries, that anyone without the right to stay may be returned through a hub, unaccompanied minors excepted. Rescue says the Commission's proposed Frontex Regulation would even let the agency support returns in third countries, subject to an EU agreement, which means common money and common oversight could follow the common accord. Five years from now, a caseworker starting out could know one procedure. The mandate wins that procedure. One policy where five patchworks now guard their own seams.

The money half is already on the table, and it points one way. The Commission has already said financing of return centres is a member-state matter, though the Union can support accompanying measures, and that stance is the lever against the resistance you can expect. UN High Commissioner for Refugees Barham Salih told the same meeting what a full policy carries: sustained funding for refugees and the countries hosting them, resettlement, education, family reunification and labour mobility across Europe. The Commission must move first: convert its observer role into a negotiating mandate this year. And you: write to your MEP and demand the vote behind it.
