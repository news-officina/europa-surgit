Brussels bought the sovereign cloud. The capitals won't plug it in.

Europe built an air-gapped cloud that no American kill switch can reach, then Scotland paused approvals and Denmark could put the data centres it needs to the back of the power queue. That has to change now.

3 November 2026

Bought the cloud, forgot the electricity. That is where Europe's digital sovereignty stands today. In April 2026, the European Commission picked Proximus, through its Clarence joint venture with LuxConnect, as one of four suppliers for a sovereign cloud framework worth up to €180 million: a six-year agreement serving the Commission, the Parliament and 70 European agencies. Brussels bought the sovereign cloud.

Start with what was built, because it is real. This joint venture runs a fully disconnected, air-gapped cloud with no external network connection, so data never leaves Luxembourg and stays outside the reach of the American Cloud Act. Two states own it: Luxembourg and Belgium. Mistral AI delivers European AI capabilities on the sovereign cloud platforms in the Cloud III ecosystem, and Thales contributes high assurance security architecture. That is a complete European stack. And the tender did more than buy it: every provider was measured against eight sovereignty criteria, and the Commission set a new benchmark for what the word sovereign actually means in practice. Europe proved it can build its own digital infrastructure, with a six-year framework worth up to €180 million behind it.

Now the part that makes no sense. A sovereign cloud without power is an empty warehouse. In September 2026, the Commission's own report said EU data centres consumed 68 TWh of electricity in 2024, projected to reach 114 TWh by 2030. STL Partners research said public opposition had impacted around $42 billion of data center investments in Europe in delays and cancellations. Scotland has paused planning approvals, and Denmark's emergency law could put data centres at the back of the queue for grid power. These blockades do not just hit the American giants they are aimed at. They hit Clarence, they hit the thousands of street cabinets that Proximus was reported in January 2026 to be planning to turn into micro data centers, and they hit you, the young European whose hospital records and public services are meant to run on one continent's computing.

The objection deserves a fair hearing, so here it is at full strength. Communities are right to resist: campaigners warned that Scotland's pause avoids repeating Ireland's situation, where power demand led to a moratorium. Local consent matters, but it cannot decide Europe's digital future on its own, so the two concerns must be kept apart. Edge computing helps: local processing means only summaries and alerts travel on the network, not raw data. What replaces the blockades is not a rubber stamp. It could be the common EU rating scheme and minimum performance standards the Commission's report outlines as possible future measures.

So here is what must happen next. The fix sits with Scotland and Denmark. Both should judge every application against the common EU rating scheme outlined in the Commission's report, and grant priority grid connections to the data centres that meet its standards and the sovereignty criteria. Five years from now, someone starting out in Brussels or Luxembourg should run code on a cloud no foreign kill switch can reach. That cloud already exists, air-gapped and under two states' ownership. The Commission has done its part. Now the capitals must plug it in.
