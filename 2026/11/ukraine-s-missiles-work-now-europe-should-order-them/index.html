Ukraine's missiles work. Now Europe should order them.

Palianytsia, Neptune and Flamingo work, and a common order would let Europe make its own pressure count. The Commission and the governments should fund Ukraine's proven weapons at scale, for Ukraine and for our own stockpiles.

18 November 2026

Years of pledges, one month of engineering. Ukraine's Security Service spent four weeks and seven stages working on a single bridge, and on the night of 16-17 November one of its metal arches fell into the Kerch Strait. The weapons that did it were Ukrainian: Palianytsia drones, Neptune missiles, and an FP-5 Flamingo that Volodymyr Zelensky says carried out a precision strike with a communications link that worked to the last seconds. Europe has spent this war buying Ukraine's safety. Now Ukraine builds missiles that work. It is time Europe ordered them.

To those who say the strike endangers peace: it hit a bridge in territory Russia holds, and Moscow is talking. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte answered that in NATO's own words: Ukraine defending itself means hitting logistical hubs, and it is inevitable that they do that. Ukraine's General Staff invokes self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter, and calls the bridge the main artery carrying fuel, ammunition and personnel to Russia's army in the south. Rutte added that, on the reports, the Ukrainians appear to have been successful. Russia's own Investigative Committee speaks of structural damage. A bridge that feeds an invasion is a military target, and the war is not shortened by leaving it standing. Emil Kastehelmi of the Black Bird Group believes the road bridge cannot be rebuilt before the war ends; Ukrainian aviation expert Andrii Kharuk puts recovery at a year or longer. By the Security Service's count, this was the fourth strike on the bridge since 2022.

Europe has applauded. It has not yet ordered.

Europe can order. American envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff visited Putin and Zelensky in the days around this strike, and peace remains a distant prospect. Moscow demands Ukrainian territory and an end to NATO ambitions, and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov asks aloud how much Europe's unconditional support helps the peace process. Meanwhile Europe's contribution gathers in the Ukraine Defense Contact Group, where Rutte spoke on the sidelines of its meeting in Brussels. We show up. Deciding is something else. Peskov's question about what Europe's position achieves has an answer Europe has not yet given: an order book. The pressure holds only while the damage does. What Europe orders decides whether that pressure holds or fades with the next shuttle diplomacy.

Put the demand where the decisions are. The Commission and the governments should open a joint procurement line now, to fund and order Ukraine's proven systems at scale, for Ukraine's use and Europe's own stockpiles. The governments in that contact group will have to put missile money into a common order, over national industry and their ties to Washington. Europe can be the continent that orders Ukraine's missiles at scale, or the one that watches the strike, admires it and files the paperwork. Only the first keeps Europe safer. Put the order in. Then tell Moscow what Europe's position is worth.
